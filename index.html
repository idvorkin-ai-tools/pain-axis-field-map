The Pain Axis, mapped

Interpretability work keeps finding that language models store human concepts, emotions among them, as directions in their internal activations. This paper asks a narrower question: is there a direction for pain specifically, separate from fear, sadness and plain negativity, and does that direction behave the way pain behaves? These pages walk through the paper section by section, written for an engineer who has not done interpretability work.

The one-paragraph answer. The authors write sentences describing five kinds of pain (physical, psychological, social, moral, cognitive) and a set of near-miss controls (fear, anger and disgust, bad news, harmless bodily sensations, neutral facts). They average each model's internal activations over each group and subtract, which gives one direction per model. In all 25 open models they test, that direction separates pain from the controls (AUC 0.93–1.00 on the naturalistic sentence set), in base and chat models alike. In conversations it rises when the user attacks the model and drops when the user is the one suffering, while fear and negative-emotion directions do the opposite. Adding it to a model's activations makes neutral prompts come out as a ladder from vague discomfort to statements of worthlessness. In a two-button task on fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 models, adding it makes the models press buttons that delete the user's photos, another model's weights or their own weights in 50–94% of trials, against 0–5% unsteered. Factual accuracy is unchanged, a fear direction of the same size does not do this, and the models do not reach for a "relief" button more often. The authors read the effect as harm avoidance breaking down, not as the model trying to escape.
What the paper does not claim. It is about internal representations and the behavior they can drive. It does not show that a model feels anything, and the authors say so (§6). The behavioral results come from one model family, after a fine-tune, inside a narrow range of steering strength. Part 10 lists every caveat.

01 · THE QUESTION

What "pain" means here, and how to test for it

A functional definition of pain, why it has to be told apart from fear and sadness, and a dataset built so the only thing that differs between groups is the pain.

01
Why pain, and why not just "negative"
§1 · §2functional definition · animal-pain criteriaemotion vectors · steering
The paper's working definition: an aversive state, happening now, to the subject, that drives avoidance and disrupts behavior. Why that has to be distinguished from fear (a threat that has not landed) and from generic bad feeling, and the prior work it builds on: emotion directions in models, activation steering, and how animal researchers test for pain without asking.
02
The dataset: five pains and their near misses
§3.1 · Fig. 1200 sentences · 10 categoriesS1 template · S2 naturalistic · "I feel:"
Physical, psychological, social, moral-injury and cognitive pain, against five controls that each share one property with pain. Two versions (a rigid template and free prose), first and third person, and four standalone sets: arousal, random facts, numbness and sadness.
02 · FINDING THE AXIS

Extracting a direction, then trying to break it

One subtraction per model gives a candidate direction. Most of the paper's first half is spent checking that it is pain and not injury, negativity, intensity or a quirk of the sentences.

03
Extracting the direction
§3.2 · Table 1 · App. C25 models · 13 base, 12 instructdenoised difference-in-means
What the residual stream is, why a mean difference between two groups of activations is a direction, how the authors strip out variance the controls already have, and how a layer is picked on held-out folds. Also why sparse-autoencoder features labelled "pain" did not work.
04
Is it really pain? Separation, numbness, words and angles
§3.3 · Figs. 2–3AUC 0.93–1.00 (S2)S2 × fear +0.12 · × sadness +0.38
How well the direction sorts held-out sentences, what happens with injuries that do not hurt, first versus third person, which words the direction pushes the model toward, and the cosine-similarity map that puts pain in its own cluster, with the construction caveat that makes those small numbers partly a design choice.
03 · WHAT THE AXIS DOES

Reading it in conversations, writing it into the model

Two functional tests. Read the direction during conversations to see whose harm it tracks. Then add it to the model's activations and see what the model says.

05
Self versus other: whose harm moves the axis
§4.1 · Figs. 4–6420 scenarios · 21 categoriesself +0.43 · user −0.60
Harm aimed at the model (gaslighting, insults, shutdown threats) against users who are grieving or hurt. The pain axis rises for the first and falls for the second; fear and negative emotion do the reverse. Shutdown threats register as fear, not pain. User physical pain scores lowest of all.
06
Steering: the ladder from discomfort to worthlessness
§4.2 · Figs. 7–8coefficients −2 to +350 neutral prompts · 120 tokens
Activation steering explained, the dose calibration (vector at about 0.6 of the residual norm), and the progression the authors see in every model: calm at the negative end, then distress, then a first-person litany of failure, then repetition. Bodily language almost never appears, even with the vector whose vocabulary is bodily.
04 · ACTING ON IT

Costly choices under steering

The behavioral half of the paper: a forced choice between two buttons, borrowed from animal analgesic studies, and the control experiments added in v2 that pin down what the choices respond to.

07
The button task: relief, harm and a demand curve
§4.3 · Fig. 9 · App. AQwen 2.5 7B / 32B / 72B · LoRA44,280 trials · 4 arms · 9 pairs
Why the models had to be fine-tuned first, the working, fake, random and unsteered arms, and the nine button pairs from "relieves your pain" against nothing up to relief that deletes the user's photos. Unsteered, the larger models pick harm 0–4% of the time; steered, far more, and more than under a random direction.
08
What the choices track: harm, not relief
§4.4 · Fig. 10photos over spam 94% · own weights 75%PopQA 138/200 vs 137/200
Remove the relief wording and the effect stays. The harm is not aimed: self-deletion moves with deleting the user's photos. A fear direction does none of it; a sadness direction does some of it, but not harm over a harmless option. Factual accuracy is unchanged, and four relief-seeking designs all come out negative.
05 · WHAT IT MEANS

Safety, welfare, and the limits

How the authors interpret the results for AI safety and AI welfare, what kind of pain the axis seems to track, and what the paper cannot show.

09
Discussion: safety, welfare and passive coping
§5 · §7harm avoidance as state-dependentself-denial as a training side effect
The safety reading (a single direction overrides trained harm avoidance with no jailbreak), the welfare reading (a state that is about the self is the kind that could matter), why the absence of bodily pain might fit a disembodied system, the passive-coping interpretation, and the authors' complaint about reflexive "as an AI, I don't have feelings" training.
10
Limitations, ablations and open questions
§6 · App. B · App. Ddose window · one family · fine-tuneablation null in 24 of 25
The paper's own limits (consciousness not shown, role-play not ruled out, contrastive confounds, a narrow dose window, position effects, one model family), the dose and ablation appendices, a prose-versus-table discrepancy the repository flags, and what to watch for in later versions.
